# Mathematical Background

This document gives a self-contained account of the theory underlying the `ergodic-bsde` library.
All notation follows the conventions used in the source code.

---

## 1. Backward Stochastic Differential Equations

### 1.1 Definition

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a complete probability space with a filtration
$(\mathcal{F}_t)_{0 \le t \le T}$ generated by a $d$-dimensional Brownian motion $(W_t)$.

A **backward stochastic differential equation (BSDE)** on $[0, T]$ with driver $f$ and
terminal condition $\xi$ is the integral equation

$$Y_t = \xi + \int_t^T f(s, Y_s, Z_s)\, ds - \int_t^T Z_s \cdot dW_s, \quad t \in [0, T].$$

A **solution** is a pair of adapted processes $(Y, Z)$ such that $Y$ is continuous and
$Z \in L^2([0,T] \times \Omega; \mathbb{R}^d)$.

- $Y_t \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **value process**.
- $Z_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the **control process** (or martingale integrand).
- $f : [0,T] \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ is the **driver** (or generator).
- $\xi \in L^2(\mathcal{F}_T)$ is the **terminal condition**.

### 1.2 Existence and Uniqueness (Pardoux–Peng 1990)

**Theorem** (Pardoux & Peng, 1990).  Suppose:

1. **(Lipschitz driver)** There exists $K > 0$ such that for all $t$, $y_1, y_2 \in \mathbb{R}$,
   $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{R}^d$:
   $$|f(t, y_1, z_1) - f(t, y_2, z_2)| \le K(|y_1 - y_2| + |z_1 - z_2|).$$
2. **(Square-integrable terminal condition)** $\xi \in L^2(\mathcal{F}_T)$.
3. **(Integrability)** $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\int_0^T |f(t,0,0)|^2\, dt\bigr] < \infty$.

Then there exists a **unique** solution $(Y, Z) \in \mathcal{S}^2 \times \mathcal{H}^2$ to the
BSDE.

The proof uses a fixed-point argument in the Banach space
$\mathcal{S}^2 \times \mathcal{H}^2$ equipped with a suitable exponentially weighted norm,
ensuring the Picard iteration is a contraction.

### 1.3 The Feynman–Kac Representation

When the state process $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a diffusion

$$dX_t = b(t, X_t)\, dt + \sigma(t, X_t)\, dW_t, \quad X_s = x,$$

and the driver and terminal condition are Markovian ($f(t, y, z)$ and $\xi = g(X_T)$), the
solution pair admits the **Feynman–Kac representation**

$$Y_t = u(t, X_t), \qquad Z_t = \sigma(t, X_t)^\top \nabla_x u(t, X_t),$$

where $u : [0, T] \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfies the **quasi-linear parabolic PDE**

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{L} u + f\bigl(t, u, \sigma^\top \nabla u\bigr) = 0,
\quad u(T, x) = g(x),$$

with generator $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma \sigma^\top \nabla^2) + b \cdot \nabla$.

This identification is the bridge between Monte Carlo BSDE simulation and PDE methods.  The library
exploits it in every solver: `Z_t = \sigma(X_t) v'(X_t)` is implemented in
`ebsde/bsde/ergodic.py` (`markovian_pde_operator`) and all PDE solvers in `ebsde/solvers/`.

## 2. Ergodic BSDEs

### 2.1 Motivation: the Long-Horizon Limit

For a fixed terminal condition $\xi$ on $[0, T]$, as $T \to \infty$ the terminal datum
$g(X_T)$ becomes negligible (the process forgets its initial condition) and one can ask what
happens to $Y_0/T$.  Under ergodicity of $X$, there is a deterministic limit $\lambda$ — the
**ergodic constant** — and a stationary correction process.

### 2.2 Definition

An **ergodic BSDE** is the equation

$$Y_t = Y_T + \int_t^T \bigl[f(X_s, Y_s, Z_s) - \lambda\bigr]\, ds
      - \int_t^T Z_s \cdot dW_s \quad \text{for all } T > t.$$

The **unknowns** are the triple $(\lambda, Y, Z)$ where:

- $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ is the **ergodic constant** (a scalar);
- $Y_t$ is a stationary adapted process (unique up to an additive constant);
- $Z_t$ is the associated martingale integrand.

The ergodic constant plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier that ensures the equation is
solvable for all time horizons simultaneously.

### 2.3 Existence (Fuhrman–Hu–Tessitore 2009)

**Theorem** (Fuhrman, Hu & Tessitore, 2009).  Suppose $X$ is an ergodic Markov diffusion with
stationary distribution $\pi$ and the driver $f$ satisfies:

1. **(Lipschitz)** $|f(x, y_1, z_1) - f(x, y_2, z_2)| \le K(|y_1 - y_2| + |z_1 - z_2|)$.
2. **(Dissipativity / monotonicity)** $f$ is non-increasing in $y$, i.e.
   $(y_1 - y_2)(f(x, y_1, z) - f(x, y_2, z)) \le -\alpha|y_1-y_2|^2$ for some $\alpha \ge 0$.
3. **(Stationarity condition)** $\mathbb{E}_\pi[|f(X, 0, 0)|] < \infty$.

Then:
- A solution $(\lambda, Y, Z)$ exists.
- Under strict dissipativity ($\alpha > 0$), $Y$ is unique.
- Without the linear $y$-term, $Y$ is unique only up to an additive constant, which is
  fixed by a **normalisation condition** such as $v(x_0) = 0$ or $\mathbb{E}_\pi[v(X)] = 0$.

For weak dissipativity assumptions (compact state space or Poincaré-inequality conditions) see
Debussche, Hu & Tessitore (2011).

### 2.4 Uniqueness of $\lambda$, Non-Uniqueness of $(Y, Z)$

A key structural feature:

> **The ergodic constant $\lambda$ is always unique.  The pair $(Y, Z)$ is unique only up to
> additive constants in $Y$.**

In the Markovian case $Y_t = v(X_t)$, adding a constant to $v$ leaves the ergodic PDE unchanged
because the constant differentiates to zero.  The normalisation $v(x_0) = 0$ (implemented in
all solvers) selects a canonical representative.

## 3. Connection to PDEs

### 3.1 The Ergodic PDE

For a **Markovian** ergodic BSDE with $Y_t = v(X_t)$ and $Z_t = \sigma(X_t) v'(X_t)$, the
pair $(v, \lambda)$ satisfies the **ergodic (elliptic) PDE**

$$\mathcal{L}v(x) + f\bigl(x, v(x), \sigma(x) \nabla v(x)\bigr) = \lambda,$$

where $\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\sigma(x)^2 \partial_{xx} + b(x)\partial_x$ is the generator.
This is a **nonlinear eigenvalue problem**: one must simultaneously find $v$ and the scalar
$\lambda$.

### 3.2 From Parabolic to Ergodic: the $T \to \infty$ Limit

The parabolic PDE for a finite-horizon BSDE is

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \mathcal{L}u + f(x, u, \sigma \nabla u) = 0, \quad u(T, x) = g(x).$$

Writing $u(t,x) = v(x) - \lambda(T-t) + w(t,x)$ where $w$ captures transient boundary-layer
effects, as $T \to \infty$ the term $w$ decays exponentially and $u(0, x)/T \to -\lambda$.
The limit $v$ satisfies the ergodic PDE above.  The rate of convergence is governed by the
spectral gap of the generator (see Section 5.5 in [ergodic_theory.md](ergodic_theory.md)).

## 4. Linear Case and Eigenvalue Problems

### 4.1 Reduction to Spectral Theory

For the **linear driver** $f(x, y, z) = h(x) + c \cdot y$ (no $z$-dependence), the ergodic
PDE reduces to

$$\mathcal{L}v(x) + h(x) + c\, v(x) = \lambda,$$

i.e.,

$$\underbrace{(\mathcal{L} + h + c\,I)}_{\displaystyle A}\, v = \lambda.$$

This is the **principal eigenvalue problem** for the operator $A$.  By Perron–Frobenius theory
(or the Krein–Rutman theorem for the infinite-dimensional case), $A$ has a unique largest
real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ with a positive eigenfunction $\phi$.  The ergodic constant equals
$\lambda_1$.

The library computes this via `PrincipalEigenvalueSolver.compute_principal_eigenvalue` in
`ebsde/applications/principal_eigenvalue.py`, using a finite-difference discretisation of $A$
followed by `scipy.linalg.eig`.

### 4.2 Donsker–Varadhan Variational Formula

The principal eigenvalue admits the **Donsker–Varadhan representation**:

$$\lambda = \sup_{\phi > 0} \inf_{x} \frac{(A\phi)(x)}{\phi(x)}
           = \sup_{\mu \in \mathcal{P}} \left[\int h\, d\mu + c - I(\mu)\right],$$

where $\mathcal{P}$ is the set of probability measures on the state space and

$$I(\mu) = \sup_{\phi > 0} \left[-\int \frac{\mathcal{L}\phi}{\phi}\, d\mu\right]$$

is the **Donsker–Varadhan rate functional** (large-deviations rate of the empirical measure of
$X$).

For the OU process, this gives the lower bound
$\lambda \ge \mathbb{E}_\pi[h(X)] + c$ (the stationary expectation) and upper bound
$\lambda \le \sup_x h(x) + c$ (the worst-case value), implemented in
`PrincipalEigenvalueSolver.donsker_varadhan_bound`.

## 5. Cole–Hopf Transform and Linearisation

### 5.1 The Quadratic Driver Case

For the driver

$$f(x, y, z) = h(x) - \frac{c}{2}|z|^2,$$

the ergodic PDE is

$$\mathcal{L}v + h(x) - \frac{c}{2}|\sigma(x) \nabla v|^2 = \lambda.$$

This is a **Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman** equation arising in risk-sensitive control and
exponential-utility optimisation.  It is nonlinear due to the $|\nabla v|^2$ term.

### 5.2 Linearisation via Cole–Hopf

The substitution

$$w(x) = \exp\bigl(-c\, v(x)\bigr)$$

transforms the nonlinear ergodic PDE into a **linear eigenvalue problem**.  Computing
derivatives:

$$\nabla v = -\frac{1}{c} \frac{\nabla w}{w}, \qquad
|\nabla v|^2 = \frac{|\nabla w|^2}{c^2 w^2}.$$

Substituting into the ergodic PDE:

$$\frac{c}{2}\frac{|\sigma \nabla w|^2}{c^2 w^2} - \frac{c}{2}\frac{|\sigma \nabla w|^2}{c^2 w^2}$$

cancels, and the equation reduces to

$$\mathcal{L}w + \frac{c}{2}\, h(x)\, w = \frac{c}{2}\, \lambda\, w,$$

which is the eigenvalue problem

$$\left(\mathcal{L} + \frac{c}{2} h\right) w = \frac{c}{2}\, \lambda\, w.$$

So $\frac{c}{2}\lambda$ is the principal eigenvalue of $\mathcal{L} + \frac{c}{2}h$, and
$w = e^{-cv}$ is the principal eigenfunction.  This identifies the quadratic ergodic BSDE
with a Schrödinger-type spectral problem, and provides an alternative computational route
via `PrincipalEigenvalueSolver`.

In the library, `quadratic_driver` implements $f(x,y,z) = h(x) - (\gamma/2)|z|^2$, and
`entropy_driver` implements $f(x,y,z) = h(x) + (1/(2\eta))|z|^2$, which after the transform
$c = -1/\eta$ becomes the same structure.

## 6. Long-Run Risk: Epstein–Zin Preferences

### 6.1 Recursive Utility

Epstein–Zin (1989) recursive utility with risk aversion $\gamma > 0$ and elasticity of
intertemporal substitution $\psi > 0$ is defined by the aggregator

$$V_t = \left[(1-e^{-\delta dt})\, C_t^{1-1/\psi}
       + e^{-\delta dt}\left(\mathbb{E}_t[V_{t+dt}^{1-\gamma}]\right)^{(1-1/\psi)/(1-\gamma)}
       \right]^{1/(1-1/\psi)},$$

where $C_t$ is consumption and $\delta$ is the subjective discount rate.

In continuous time, the continuation value $J_t$ satisfies a backward SDE with driver
encoding the certainty-equivalent adjustment.  In the **log-linearised** approximation around
the stationary distribution of consumption growth $X_t$ (which follows an OU process), the
driver takes the form

$$f(x, v, z) = \delta\,\theta_{\mathrm{EZ}}\, x + \frac{\gamma}{2}|z|^2,$$

where the **Epstein–Zin aggregator coefficient** is

$$\theta_{\mathrm{EZ}} = \frac{1-\gamma}{1-1/\psi}.$$

The ergodic constant $\lambda$ is then the **long-run risk-adjusted discount rate**.

### 6.2 Parameter Interpretation

| Parameter | Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Risk aversion | $\gamma$ | Curvature of static utility; $\gamma > 1/\psi$ → risk-aversion dominates |
| EIS | $\psi$ | Intertemporal substitution; $\psi > 1$ → agent prefers early resolution |
| Discount rate | $\delta$ | Impatience |
| EZ coefficient | $\theta_{\mathrm{EZ}}$ | Sign: positive if $\gamma > 1$ and $\psi > 1$ simultaneously |

When $\gamma = 1/\psi$ (CRRA power utility), $\theta_{\mathrm{EZ}} = 1$ and the model
reduces to the standard expected utility case.  Implemented in
`LongRunRiskPricer` (`ebsde/applications/long_run_risk.py`).

## 7. Hansen–Scheinkman Decomposition

### 7.1 The Decomposition

Hansen & Scheinkman (2009) showed that any multiplicative functional (pricing kernel) $M_T$
can be decomposed as

$$M_T = e^{-\lambda T}\, \frac{\phi(X_T)}{\phi(X_0)}\, L_T,$$

where:
- $\lambda$ is the **long-run growth rate** of $M$ (the ergodic constant);
- $\phi > 0$ is the **principal eigenfunction** of the pricing operator $A = \mathcal{L} + h + cI$;
- $L_T$ is a **martingale** under the original measure $\mathbb{P}$.

The decomposition separates the **permanent** component $e^{-\lambda T} \phi(X_T)/\phi(X_0)$
(which grows at the long-run rate and is entirely captured by the eigenvalue–eigenfunction pair)
from the **transient martingale** component $L_T$ (which averages to 1 and carries no long-run
growth).

### 7.2 Connection to the Ergodic BSDE

Setting $v(x) = \log \phi(x)$, the eigenvalue equation $A\phi = \lambda\phi$ reads

$$\mathcal{L}v + \frac{1}{2}|\sigma \nabla v|^2 + h + c = \lambda,$$

which is precisely the ergodic PDE with quadratic driver
$f(x, y, z) = h(x) + c - \frac{1}{2}|z|^2$ (after Cole–Hopf back-transformation).

Implemented in `PrincipalEigenvalueSolver.hansen_scheinkman_decomposition`, which:
1. Computes $(\lambda, \phi)$ via the FD eigenvalue solver;
2. Simulates forward paths $X_t$;
3. Estimates $L_T$ via the formula above and checks $\mathbb{E}[L_T] \approx 1$.
